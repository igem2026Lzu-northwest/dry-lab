# Model Assumptions & Limitations

Our Dry Lab modeling is based on genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) and Monod dynamic ODEs. To ensure scientific integrity and reproducibility, we explicitly acknowledge the following assumptions and limitations. These factors are critical for correctly interpreting our simulation results, especially the negative results. Model predictions should be interpreted as computational evidence and hypotheses, not as experimental validation.

## 1. Lactate Production (Flux Balance Analysis)

*   **Steady-State Assumption**: Standard FBA assumes intracellular metabolite concentrations reach a steady state, ignoring dynamic regulatory mechanisms, enzyme expression kinetics, and metabolic adaptation over time.
*   **Yield Discrepancy (Apparent Yield > 2.0)**: The apparent lactate yield in Stage 2 is 2.17 mol/mol glucose when glucose is used as the sole carbon-input denominator. This value is higher than the theoretical maximum of 2.0 when only glucose is considered. The reason is that the model incorporates trace carbon-containing cofactors (e.g., lipoate, glutathione, B-vitamins) as supplementary carbon sources. When all supplemented carbon-containing cofactors are included in the total carbon input, the carbon-based yield is approximately 0.98 mol C/mol C, which is close to the theoretical carbon limit. The two values use different denominators; when interpreted consistently, neither violates carbon conservation. We recommend including a small carbon-balance table in the supplementary materials to make this distinction explicit.
*   **Idealized Environment**: The model does not account for lactate toxicity, pH feedback inhibition, mass transfer limitations, or cell death at high product titers. These factors may reduce actual lactate production and should be considered in wet-lab validation.

## 2. Tyrosine Pathway (Melanin Synthesis)

*   **Non-Growth Associated Product**: Standard FBA cannot naturally drive the synthesis of non-essential secondary metabolites (melanin) because the solver is designed to maximize growth. We bypassed this by applying a forced lower bound (`EX_melanin_e ≥ 0.1`), which simulates strong promoter induction (e.g., IPTG) in wet lab experiments. This is a modeling intervention rather than a prediction of natural pathway behavior.
*   **TYRTA Directionality**: In silico overexpression of TYRTA (an aminotransferase) did not show a strong metabolic burden in our FBA simulations. However, FBA cannot capture enzyme directionality, regulation, or expression burden. Biological evidence suggests that TYRTA tends to consume tyrosine (converting it to 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate) rather than producing it. Therefore, we recommend that wet lab teams consider feedback-resistant mutants (e.g., `tyrA_fbr`) instead of relying solely on TYRTA overexpression. This recommendation is based on pathway logic and literature, and should be experimentally validated.
*   **Lethality of pheA Knockout**: The lethal phenotype of `ΔpheA` is highly dependent on the external supply of phenylalanine and tyrosine. In a rich medium, the knockout might survive; our model specifically simulated a restricted exogenous amino acid environment to mimic a minimal gut environment. The lethality conclusion should therefore be interpreted within this specific condition.

## 3. Gut Microbiome GEM (Monod ODE & SteadyCom)

*   **Substrate Utilization Breadth**: Under relaxed FBA conditions derived from the AGORA database, most bacteria can utilize all tested short-chain fatty acids. This leads to the "substrate hijacking" phenomenon observed in our model, where commensal/pathogenic bacteria (e.g., *Bth*, *Eco*) outcompete probiotics for lactate. This is a current limitation of the database parameters, but it also reflects the fierce competition in the gut. The hijacking result should be treated as a model prediction; sensitivity analysis under alternative substrate-utilization assumptions is recommended.
*   **Spatial Structure Neglect**: The ODE model assumes a well-mixed environment (like a chemostat) and ignores spatial structures, such as the mucosal layer, biofilm formation, and the physical distance between the engineered probiotic and target epithelial cells.
*   **Absence of Veillonella**: The 16S rRNA data from the irradiated mice lacks the genus *Veillonella*, which is known to convert lactate to propionate. The absence of this key species in our model may cause lactate to accumulate abnormally, potentially amplifying the competitive advantage of lactate-consuming pathogens and strengthening the negative result. Therefore, the "lactate hijacking" conclusion should be interpreted with this caveat. Adding *Veillonella* or performing sensitivity analysis on lactate utilization would help test the robustness of this prediction.
*   **Parameter Uncertainty**: The initial abundances and `mu_max` values are derived from 16S sequencing data and the AGORA database, not from individual experimental validation for our specific engineered strain. These parameters should be treated as approximate and refined when experimental data become available.

## 4. Overall Strategy: From Negative Results to Engineering Insights

Despite the aforementioned limitations, the model-based negative result regarding lactate hijacking provides a useful engineering insight: **direct oral supplementation of lactate may be inefficient due to global ecological competition.** This result supports, rather than experimentally proves, the rationale for our engineering strategy: **targeted, in-situ lactate secretion by engineered probiotics**. By bypassing the upper gastrointestinal tract and releasing lactate directly at the mucosal surface, our system is designed to minimize competition with commensal bacteria and maximize the Wnt-activating and microbiota-regulating effects. Experimental validation will be required to confirm whether this strategy performs as predicted.
